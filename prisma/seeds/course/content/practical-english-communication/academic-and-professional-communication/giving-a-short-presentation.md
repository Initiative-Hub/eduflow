## Giving a Short Presentation

A short presentation succeeds when the audience can follow one central message from opening to conclusion. Clear organization and practiced delivery matter more than placing every fact on slides.

### Design the message

Decide what the audience should understand or do afterward. Then choose two or three supporting points. If a point does not support the central message, leave it out.

Useful opening structure:

1. State the topic.
2. Explain why it matters.
3. Preview the main points.

Avoid beginning with a long apology or a complete history of the topic.

### Guide the audience

Signposting language tells listeners where they are.

- "First, I will explain..."
- "The key difference is..."
- "Now let us look at an example."
- "To summarize..."

Slides should reinforce structure with readable evidence. They should not duplicate the entire script.

### Rehearse for communication

Practice aloud with short notes. Mark important words for emphasis. Time the whole talk. Rehearsal helps you notice sentences that look clear on paper but are difficult to say.

### Delivery checklist

- [ ] The opening explains relevance.
- [ ] Main points are limited and ordered.
- [ ] Slides are readable.
- [ ] Notes use keywords, not full paragraphs.
- [ ] The conclusion repeats the main message.

> A presentation is for the audience, not for proving how much information you collected.

### Guided practice

Prepare a three-minute presentation explaining one useful idea from this course. Use no more than three slides, record one rehearsal, and revise the opening or conclusion based on playback.

### Key takeaway

A confident short presentation gives the audience one clear destination and guides them there.
