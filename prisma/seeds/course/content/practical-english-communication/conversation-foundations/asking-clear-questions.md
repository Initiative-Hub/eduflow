## Asking Clear Questions

Questions help people exchange information, confirm meaning, and make decisions. A clear question uses the right shape, accurate word order, and enough context for the listener to answer efficiently.

### Choose the question type

Open questions invite explanation. Closed questions confirm a specific fact. Follow-up questions develop an idea after the first answer.

- Open: "What was the most difficult part of the assignment?"
- Closed: "Did you submit the assignment yesterday?"
- Follow-up: "What made that part difficult?"

Use open questions when you need detail. Use closed questions when you need confirmation. Use follow-ups when the first answer is not enough.

### Direct and indirect questions

Direct questions often invert the subject and auxiliary verb.

- "Where are you working?"
- "When does the meeting start?"

Indirect questions are more polite or formal, but the word order after the opening becomes statement order.

- "Could you tell me where you are working?"
- "Do you know when the meeting starts?"

### Add useful context

Vague questions create extra messages. Name the document, date, task, or decision you mean.

Instead of "Can you check it?" say "Can you check the introduction paragraph in my draft before Friday afternoon?"

### Question repair

If the listener looks confused, repair the question instead of repeating the same sentence louder.

1. Add context.
2. Break the question into two parts.
3. Give an example answer.
4. Ask whether a specific part is unclear.

> Politeness helps, but clarity should not be buried under a long apology.

### Guided practice

Rewrite five vague questions from school or work. For two of them, write a direct version and a polite indirect version. Then ask a partner which version feels more appropriate for the situation.

### Key takeaway

A clear question matches the information you need and gives the listener enough context to answer well.
