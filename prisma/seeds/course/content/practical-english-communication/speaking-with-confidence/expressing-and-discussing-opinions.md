## Expressing and Discussing Opinions

Productive discussion connects an opinion to reasons and evidence while leaving room for other perspectives. Good language helps people disagree with ideas without attacking people.

### Calibrate certainty

Choose phrases that match how strong your evidence is.

- Low certainty: "It seems that..."
- Personal experience: "In my experience..."
- Moderate certainty: "I think..."
- Strong certainty: "I am convinced that..."

Overly strong language can sound careless if the evidence is limited. Very weak language can hide a useful point.

### Build a support chain

A strong opinion usually includes a claim, a reason, and an example.

```text
Claim: Online practice should be short and frequent.
Reason: Learners remember more when practice is repeated.
Example: Ten minutes every day helped me review vocabulary better than one long session.
```

This structure gives listeners something specific to evaluate.

### Agree and disagree respectfully

You can acknowledge one part before adding a concern.

- "I agree that it is faster, but I am not sure it is more reliable."
- "That may work for beginners. For advanced learners, I would add more choice."
- "I see your point. My concern is the cost."

### Discussion questions

- "What evidence supports that?"
- "Can you give an example?"
- "Are we using the same definition?"
- "What would happen in a different context?"

> Good disagreement keeps the focus on the claim, reason, and evidence.

### Guided practice

Choose a familiar topic. Prepare one opinion, two reasons, and one example. Then respond to an opposing view using partial agreement and one follow-up question.

### Key takeaway

Strong discussion combines a supported position with language that keeps other perspectives open.
