## Editing for Clarity

Editing separates idea generation from reader-focused revision. A useful process checks structure first, then sentence clarity, word choice, and final correctness.

### Edit from large to small

Do not start with commas if the paragraph order is unclear. First check the purpose, audience, organization, and missing information. Polishing a sentence that will later be deleted wastes time.

1. Purpose: Is the main point clear?
2. Organization: Does the order help the reader?
3. Paragraphs: Does each paragraph develop one idea?
4. Sentences: Are they clear and direct?
5. Correctness: Are spelling, punctuation, and format accurate?

### Strengthen sentences

Prefer concrete subjects and active verbs when they improve clarity.

Weak: "There was a decision made by the team to delay the launch."

Stronger: "The team decided to delay the launch."

Remove repeated ideas, empty openings, and inflated phrases. Choose precise words over impressive words.

### Proofread with a method

Proofreading is easier when you look for one type of issue at a time.

- Read once for missing words.
- Read once for verb tense.
- Read once for punctuation.
- Check names, dates, file names, and links separately.

Tools can help, but they should not make final decisions for you.

> Editing is not only correcting errors. It is improving the reader's path through the message.

### Guided practice

Revise a 150-word draft in three passes: organization, sentence clarity, and correctness. Save each version and write one sentence explaining the highest-impact change.

### Key takeaway

Efficient editing moves from purpose and structure toward sentences and correctness.
